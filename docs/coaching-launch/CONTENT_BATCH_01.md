## Post 1 — footage: choose a manageable starting session

Purpose: help a beginner turn an uncertain first visit into a specific next step. Draft duration: 30–40 seconds.

Shot list:

1. Peter facing camera, 0–5s: “Starting strength training? Make your first session something you can repeat.” Text: “A starting point you can repeat”.
2. Peter preparing an uncomplicated exercise demonstration of his choice, 5–15s: “Choose a small number of movements you understand, and use a load you can control.” Avoid prescribing a universal load or teaching an unfamiliar movement in a few seconds.
3. Close-up of a fictional paper log, 15–25s: “Write down the exercise, the load, and what you completed. That gives your next session a starting point.” Show fictional entries with no names.
4. Peter to camera, 25–35s: “Your schedule and equipment matter. If you want to discuss a starting plan, use the contact form on my website.”

Caption draft: “A first strength session can start with a simple question: what can I practice and repeat? Pick movements you understand, use a manageable load, and record what you completed. This is a general demonstration; your starting point depends on your experience and circumstances. Want to discuss your goals, equipment, and schedule? Use the website contact form. An inquiry does not book or purchase coaching.”

Cover: “Your first session: keep a record”. Alt-text draft: “Peter demonstrates preparing a training exercise and recording a fictional session on paper.” Revise alt text to match the actual footage.

## Post 2 — footage: what a training log tells you

Purpose: show a concrete beginner workflow without promising software access. Draft duration: 30–40 seconds.

Shot list:

1. Peter to camera, 0–5s: “Can you remember exactly what you did last session?”
2. Paper log or generic fictional spreadsheet, 5–15s: show exercise, load, repetitions, and a short note. “A short record helps you compare sessions.”
3. Peter repeats the same comfortable demonstration, 15–25s: “The number alone does not tell the whole story. Note what felt manageable and what you want to ask about.” Do not imply measured force, velocity, or readiness from footage.
4. Peter to camera, 25–35s: “If you are unsure how to organize your training, tell me about your starting point through the website contact form.”

Caption draft: “A training log can be simple: exercise, load, repetitions, and a short note. Use it to remember what happened and prepare questions for your next session. The example here is fictional. I’m inviting conversations about beginner strength training; software access and program purchases are not available from the website yet. Contact me through the website form if you would like to discuss coaching.”

Cover: “Remember your last session”. Alt-text draft: “Peter demonstrates a training movement beside a fictional exercise log.” Update to the actual final edit. Do not show an unreleased app as available to customers.

## Post 3 — carousel: four things to bring to a coaching conversation

Purpose: make inquiries easier to answer. Use five slides with large readable type and one idea each.

1. “New to strength training? Bring these four things to a coaching conversation.”
2. “Your goal. What would you like to learn or become more consistent at?”
3. “Your starting point. Tell me what training you have tried and what feels confusing.”
4. “Your equipment and time. Where will you train, and which days can you realistically use?”
5. “Your questions. Ask about the plan, check-ins, communication, price, and cancellation terms before deciding. Start with the website contact form. An inquiry is not a booking.”

Caption draft: “You do not need a perfect training history to ask about coaching. A useful first conversation starts with your goal, current experience, available equipment, and realistic schedule. Ask what is included and how the arrangement works before agreeing to anything. Use the contact form on my website to start a conversation. Keep sensitive personal information out of public comments.”

Alt text: transcribe each slide's text and describe any meaningful accompanying illustration. Decorative shapes need no invented description. No client images or testimonials are needed.
